1. San Antonio Spurs15–3 · ↔ No. 1

4DK thesis — The best team in our rankings is the one that can win in more than one way. San Antonio's 15–3 start is the clearest reason we keep the Spurs atop the Power 30, but the more interesting question is what the record says about their structure. Victor Wembanyama gives the Spurs an advantage that cannot be reduced to a single box-score category. His length changes the geometry of an opponent's attack: drivers have to consider whether their normal finishing window exists, while perimeter defenders can pressure the ball with greater confidence when the back line has a legitimate eraser. The Spurs cannot mistake that protection for permission to lose assignments. Their best defensive possessions still require the guards to fight through screens, the wings to rotate on time and the entire group to finish with a rebound.

Offensively, the challenge is to avoid becoming a collection of players waiting for Wembanyama to solve every possession. San Antonio is at its most difficult to guard when his screening, interior threat and passing create decisions for the defense before the ball reaches his hands. Stephon Castle's 28 points, eight rebounds and 11 assists in Monday's 123–107 victory over Sacramento illustrate why the supporting cast matters. The Spurs won that game while Wembanyama and De'Aaron Fox rested, which is an unusually persuasive piece of evidence for a No. 1 ranking: they did not need their MVP frontrunner to dominate the scoreboard to handle business. A 19-point opening quarter left them behind early, but a 41-point second quarter flipped the game; the response mattered more than the initial deficit.

What could undo them: teams with physical centers, patient ball movement and enough shooting to punish aggressive help will test whether San Antonio's defense can remain connected for 48 minutes. Their youth also makes late-game offensive discipline a real playoff question. 4DK verdict: The Spurs are not first simply because Wembanyama is our MVP leader. They're first because the roster is starting to show that its identity survives when he sits. That's the difference between a spectacular star-driven team and a serious contender.

2. Los Angeles Lakers15–3 · ↔ No. 2

4DK thesis — Luka makes the offense dangerous; the rest of the roster determines how far that danger travels. The Lakers have matched San Antonio's 15–3 record, and their 109–94 win over Utah pushed their recent form to 11 victories in 12 games. Luka Dončić remains the organizer of almost everything opponents fear: he can manipulate a screen defender with pace, turn a switch into a mismatch, or hold the ball long enough to force help and then find the release valve. But the difference between a great Luka possession and a great Lakers offense is whether the other four players know where the next advantage is supposed to come from.

Monday's win was revealing precisely because Luka's 28 points came on 8-for-24 shooting and 3-for-14 from deep. Los Angeles did not need him to be an efficient jump shooter to win comfortably. The Lakers rebounded, limited Utah to 94 points and got meaningful work from role players. That is a better sign for postseason sustainability than another explosive scoring night would have been. The offensive ceiling remains tied to Luka's shot creation, but the defensive floor depends on perimeter containment, collective rebounding and how consistently the group avoids giving away transition chances after a miss or turnover.

The tension: Luka's methodical control is a weapon, yet the Lakers must still find pace and movement when defenses load two defenders toward him. Over a playoff series, an opponent will test the spacing of every lineup and make the supporting cast prove it can finish advantages. 4DK verdict: No. 2 is not a punishment for sharing the league's best record. We are asking which contender has shown the most complete two-way identity, and San Antonio narrowly leads. The Lakers are close enough that one high-level head-to-head performance could reshape the debate.

The Sixers need the players around him to create a second and third action, not stand still waiting for a difficult rescue shot. On defense, the standard for a top-three Power 30 team is not merely having capable individual stoppers; it is communicating through repeated screening actions and preventing one breakdown from becoming an uncontested shot or a second-chance opportunity.

The tension: Philadelphia has earned the benefit of its 14 wins, but the top of the East will not be decided by record alone. Against versatile wings and fast decisions, the Sixers must show that their defensive coverage is as adaptable as their offensive talent. 4DK verdict: We move Philadelphia up because the larger body of work remains elite. The Portland loss is not a reason to panic; it is a useful test case for whether this team can identify a weakness before an opponent makes it a playoff blueprint.

4. Houston Rockets13–5 · ↑ 1

4DK thesis — Houston's ceiling depends on turning two very different offensive superpowers into one coherent system. Kevin Durant's shotmaking and Alperen Şengün's passing attack defenses from different directions. Durant can punish a defender even when the initial action produces only a sliver of space. Şengün is more likely to force a defense into a chain reaction: a handoff, a cutter, a second-side pass, a center stepping away from the rim at the wrong time. The Rockets are most dangerous when those styles complement one another rather than taking turns. A team that can get a high-quality shot without asking Durant to beat a double-team every trip becomes considerably harder to scheme against.

Houston's 116–104 win over the Clippers supplied a direct example of Durant's value. He scored 32 points and hit three late three-pointers, turning an opponent's hope of a comeback into a reminder that a great scorer can still decide the end of a game. The more demanding question for the Rockets is how much of the preceding 43 minutes they can control through defense, rebounding and Şengün-led organization. Their 13–5 record says the structure is working, but the postseason will test whether they can generate efficient looks when opponents take away their preferred handoffs and force quicker decisions from secondary ballhandlers.

The tension: Durant's brilliance can cover an ugly possession, but it cannot be the entire late-game playbook. Houston also has to defend without sacrificing its ability to finish the possession on the glass. 4DK verdict: The Rockets deserve the No. 4 spot because they have the personnel to win through both execution and elite individual shotmaking. Their next step is proving the two can coexist against top-tier playoff defenses for an entire series.

Those habits could make Minnesota more dangerous once Edwards returns, provided the team does not immediately abandon them. Defensively, the Wolves' size and versatility are supposed to be the foundation. Scoring 142 against a struggling opponent is encouraging, but it does not tell us whether Minnesota can consistently contain a contender's first action and defend the second one without fouling.

The tension: Edwards is still the player who can lift the ceiling in a late playoff possession. The current supporting-cast growth should reduce his burden, not convince the Wolves they no longer need him. 4DK verdict: No. 5 rewards a team that has answered adversity with substance. The next level is marrying this shared offensive confidence to the defensive edge that can make Minnesota a genuine championship threat.

The tension: The seven-game streak is real; so is the need to prove it against the deepest Western Conference offenses and over multiple playoff adjustments. 4DK verdict: Miami's rise to No. 6 is one of the biggest stories of this edition. We are buying the fit and the defensive possibilities, while holding back from calling the Heat a finished contender until their spacing and late-game counters face sustained elite opposition.

7. Denver Nuggets10–5 · ↑ 5

4DK thesis — Denver's offense remains a problem because Jokić changes what counts as an open shot. Nikola Jokić can find an advantage that does not look available when a possession begins. He reads a defender's body position, the timing of a cut and the hesitation of the weak-side helper, then makes the pass that turns those small mistakes into points. Jamal Murray is vital to that structure because his two-man chemistry with Jokić forces opponents to choose between protecting the paint and giving a capable shot creator room to operate. Denver's 10–5 record is less flashy than the leaders' marks, but the quality of its offensive organization makes it a serious threat.

Jokić's Western Conference Player of the Week stretch — 28.8 points, 12 rebounds and 9.5 assists per game for November 16–22 — is the kind of production that can tempt analysts to stop at the numbers. We should go further: those assists reflect Denver's dependence on players cutting decisively, making shots and reading the same defense. When the spacing is right, the Nuggets can make an opponent defend the whole floor. When it is not, even Jokić can be left choosing between an awkward attempt and a pass to a teammate who is not ready.

The tension: Denver's defensive ceiling is less automatic than its offensive one. The Nuggets have to manage transition defense, contain quick guards and avoid letting opponents target unfavorable switches repeatedly. 4DK verdict: A five-place rise is justified because Denver has the most reliable half-court offensive brain in basketball. To climb into our top five, the Nuggets must demonstrate that the defensive side can withstand the same playoff scrutiny as their beautiful offense.

4DK verdict: A six-place rise rewards the results and the improved direction, not a declaration that New York has solved everything. Rookie #8 — Aday Mara | Oklahoma City Thunder | —

The 4DK thesis: Mara is a reminder that rookie value is not confined to perimeter scoring. His November 24 snapshot lists 10.0 points, 7.1 rebounds and 1.9 assists, along with 1.7 blocks per game. His size can change an opponent's choices near the basket, while a well-timed screen or interior pass can create offense without appearing in a scoring highlight. Oklahoma City needs that kind of connective play, particularly when injuries leave its established creators carrying heavier burdens.

The developmental question is whether Mara can stay effective when opponents force him to defend away from the rim. A center can be excellent at protecting the paint and still be targeted by quick guards in space. His screening angles, foul discipline and ability to make the next pass will also determine how many offensive minutes he can earn. 4DK verdict: No. 8 recognizes the difficulty of his defensive assignment and the utility of a true interior presence. To move higher, he must show that his strengths survive different matchups and that he can contribute without requiring the offense to slow down around him.

Rookie #9 — Brayden Burries | Milwaukee Bucks | ↑1

The 4DK thesis: Burries is moving up because his contributions are becoming harder to ignore, even while Milwaukee's season is going badly. His November 24 snapshot lists 11.4 points, 5.7 rebounds and 2.5 assists. That rebounding figure is meaningful for a perimeter-oriented rookie: securing the ball can end a defensive possession and give his team a chance to run before the opponent gets organized. The challenge is converting activity into dependable offensive value rather than letting the occasional strong game mask uneven decision-making.

On a struggling team, young players can receive both more opportunities and less structure. Burries has to learn to choose when to attack, when to move the ball and when a possession simply needs a disciplined shot rather than a difficult attempt. Defensively, his rebounding only reaches full value if he has first completed the coverage assignment that allows the team to contest the shot. 4DK verdict: His rise from tenth to ninth is measured, not a declaration that he has solved Milwaukee's problems. We are rewarding useful all-around production and asking for a more consistent role as the season develops.

Rookie #10 — Mikel Brown Jr. | Brooklyn Nets | NEW

Brown's entrance into our Rookie Watch is about a changing job description. Brooklyn's record doesn't make a compelling winning-impact argument, so we shouldn't pretend it does. What we can point to is his first career start against Minnesota: 17 points, five rebounds and seven assists. Seven assists in a first start are meaningful because they show he wasn't being asked only to hunt his own shot; the Nets gave him enough on-ball responsibility to involve other players and organize possessions. One game is not a trend, but it is an evidence-based reason to examine his role more closely.

The next step is not merely scoring 20. We want to see how he handles opponents pressuring his dribble, whether he can consistently find a second-side option after the first action is stopped, and whether his shot selection holds up when the game slows down. That is where a rookie guard turns opportunity into trust. Brown earns our No. 10 position over Keaton Wagler, whose recent four-game stretch featured 7.8 points per game on 31.3% shooting, but this isn't a verdict on either player's long-term ceiling. 4DK verdict: Brown enters on role growth and playmaking evidence; he stays only if the decisions and efficiency catch up with the opportunity.
