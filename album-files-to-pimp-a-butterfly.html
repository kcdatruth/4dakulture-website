TO PIMP A BUTTERFLY.

Eleven years later, Kendrick Lamar’s fearless collision of jazz, funk, hip-hop and Black American life remains a masterpiece that refused to play by anybody else’s rules.

THE 4DK RETROSPECTIVE · 11 YEARS LATER

AFTER GOOD KID, WHAT NEXT?

Following good kid, m.A.A.d city, Kendrick Lamar could have taken the obvious route: bigger hooks, a cleaner radio formula, famous producers lined up for blockbuster singles. By 2015, he had already proven he could construct a deeply personal, cinematic rap album that connected outside of Compton. The question was whether he would repeat the blueprint or risk alienating the audience he had just won.

He took the risk. To Pimp a Butterfly is not a conventional victory lap. It is the sound of an artist examining the forces that helped lift him up while wondering what those same forces might take from him. Fame and exploitation. Community and survivor’s guilt. Black pride and the fractures inside Black life. Depression, faith, temptation, and the uneasy weight of being called a leader.

Eleven years later, that ambition still feels startling. Some tracks make you dance before revealing what they are really saying. Others barely pretend to be comfortable. And the sequencing makes nearly every detour matter. We gave this project 9.8/10 not because every track works as a traditional single, but because the album works as one of hip-hop’s most fully realized statements.

THE SOUND: A WHOLE BAND, NOT A CHECKLIST

Flying Lotus, Thundercat, Terrace Martin, Sounwave, Kamasi Washington, Robert Glasper and other musicians helped create a musical environment that feels alive. Horns push into one another. Basslines twist around the drums. Jazz phrasing crashes into G-funk and P-Funk. Soul harmonies interrupt Kendrick’s tightest rapping. The record’s palette can be gorgeous, abrasive, playful or exhausting, sometimes inside the same song.

It draws from the Black musical tradition without treating that tradition like a museum exhibit. George Clinton’s presence on “Wesley’s Theory” is a bridge between eras. The Knxwledge pocket on “Momma” gives the record underground texture. Ronald Isley’s contribution to “How Much a Dollar Cost” brings its moral reckoning into focus. Kendrick invited players with their own musical identities to help him make something that could not be reduced to a playlist of fashionable beats.

THE WEST COAST NEVER LEFT THE ROOM

Let’s get one thing straight: all that jazz experimentation does not mean Kendrick forgot Compton. “King Kunta,” “Hood Politics” and “You Ain’t Gotta Lie” carry California in their rhythmic DNA. When we heard “King Kunta,” our comparison went straight to DJ Quik and Mausberg — that late-’90s, early-2000s pocket where the bass, drums and voice are all part of the same bounce. That resemblance is part of the song’s creative lineage, not an accidental similarity.

The album often sounds like the West Coast talking to its ancestors: funk bands, spoken-word poets, the Isley Brothers, Snoop Dogg, and the memory of 2Pac. It asks what the next generation is supposed to do with all that history. That is a much bigger challenge than simply sounding nostalgic.

THE ALBUM’S HIDDEN STRUCTURE

Listen to the emotional movement: “Wesley’s Theory” opens with the economics of success; “Institutionalized” brings the past into the room; “These Walls” sounds heavenly while telling a darker story; “u” collapses into self-accusation; “Alright” answers with survival. Lucy’s temptation hangs over “For Sale?,” and the road back to self-understanding winds through “Momma,” “Complexion,” and “i.”

Then there is that recurring poem, arriving in pieces until “Mortal Man” lays its argument out in full. You can enjoy individual records, of course. But this is an album that rewards being played from start to finish, exactly in order. The two interludes are NR as conventional songs for 4DK, but each earns 10/10 for sequencing and musical execution. That distinction is deliberate.

These are the exact ratings we locked in through the 4DK listening session. The two interludes remain NR as conventional songs, with 10/10 sequencing scores. The overall album grade stays 9.8/10.

01
THE OPENING STATEMENT 4DK PICK ★

Wesley’s Theory

feat. George Clinton & Thundercat
9.4/ 10

Kendrick doesn’t waste a second announcing that this will not be a sequel to good kid, m.A.A.d city. A Boris Gardiner fragment opens the door; then Flying Lotus and Thundercat pull us into a dense P-Funk universe with George Clinton in the building. The bass twists, the arrangement refuses to stay still, and Kendrick sounds ready to attack a whole new canvas.

The song is catchy enough to move to, but the subject is anything but light: the machinery waiting to profit when a young Black artist finally gets paid. Our first reaction was the same one we had while rating it: the beat and introduction are hard, and the experiment starts immediately. This sets up the butterfly’s central struggle — what fame can give you, and what it can take away.

02
THE JAZZ POETRY BREAK

For Free? (Interlude)

NR10/10 SEQUENCING

Treating this like a normal single misses the point. Robert Glasper’s restless piano and the band’s stop-start intensity make the arrangement feel like a spoken-word session that escaped the stage and went straight into the studio. Kendrick’s phrasing is frantic, theatrical, deliberately excessive — and completely in control.

We are leaving it not rated as a conventional song, while giving the musical execution and placement a perfect 10. Following “Wesley’s Theory,” it expands the album’s argument about exploitation and worth. The poetry, the jazz tribute, the raw performance energy: this is the kind of interlude that makes the next track hit harder.

03
THE WEST COAST ANTHEM 4DK PICK ★

King Kunta

9.6/ 10

Here comes the bassline, the drums and the attitude. Sounwave and the musicians strip the funk down to something direct and nasty, while Kendrick steps into the song with his chest out. It’s one of the album’s most immediate records, but the swagger comes with a larger argument about who gets to hold power — and who tries to cut that power down.

Our 4DK comparison was to an updated DJ Quik and Mausberg record, and the connection runs deeper than a casual resemblance: that era of California funk helped shape the track’s direction. It sounds like a lineage being passed forward, not nostalgia for nostalgia’s sake. An undeniable single that still feels tied to the larger concept.

04
THE MIND NEVER MOVED OUT 4DK PICK ★

Institutionalized

feat. Bilal, Anna Wise & Snoop Dogg
9.9/ 10

One of the strongest songs Kendrick has ever written. The beat is gorgeous, the bars are surgically precise, and the shifting voices make the story move like a movie. Bilal’s soulful presence gives the hook exactly the right atmosphere; Anna Wise and Snoop Dogg add texture without pulling attention away from the story.

Kendrick examines the feeling of stepping into elite rooms while still carrying the conditioning of where you came from. Material success doesn’t erase fear, loyalty conflicts, pride or survival instincts. That truth resonates beyond rap. For us, this is a personal favorite and the first near-perfect score of the record. It’s an extraordinary combination of message and musicality.

05
THE PERFECT BLEND 4DK PICK ★

These Walls

feat. Bilal, Anna Wise & Thundercat
10.0/ 10

This one is flawless to our ears. The warm bass, layered harmonies and irresistible groove make it one of the smoothest Kendrick records ever, and its placement immediately after “Institutionalized” is beautiful. Bilal, Anna Wise and Thundercat help create a silky musical surface that almost convinces you you’re hearing a carefree love song.

But then the writing pulls you deeper into the consequences of intimacy, incarceration, revenge and guilt. The sweetness of the production intensifies the darkness rather than hiding it. It’s easy to enjoy casually and rewarding to study closely. A banger, a perfect spot on the album, and our first 10/10.

06
A BREAKDOWN ON RECORD

u

9.5/ 10

We are not going to pretend this clicked immediately. The opening half is chaotic, his delivery almost unbearable by design, and the song can be difficult to sit through until you understand why it sounds that way. Kendrick is performing a breakdown, not polishing away its rough edges.

Then comes the switch. The more intimate second half lets the self-accusations settle into focus, and the arrangement makes the pain easier to follow without making it less intense. Coming after the lushness of “These Walls,” the transition suddenly feels essential. It took repeated listens for us to unlock it — and that’s part of the genius.

07
HOPE WITH THE WORLD ON ITS SHOULDERS

Alright

9.7/ 10

After “u” turns inward toward despair, “Alright” pushes outward toward survival. The Pharrell and Sounwave production is lively and propulsive, giving Kendrick a sound that can command a festival crowd without disconnecting from the album’s jazz and funk language. The verses remain sharp even as the hook lifts everything around them.

The record became a powerful expression of hope during a period of protest against racial injustice. That cultural role is real, but so is the music itself: one of Kendrick’s defining classics, a record that resonates in a real way. The move from “u” to “Alright” may be the record’s most emotionally important two-song sequence.

08
LUCY ENTERS THE ROOM

For Sale? (Interlude)

NR10/10 SEQUENCING

This interlude floats instead of charges. The voices are feather-light, the arrangement is psychedelic and the whole atmosphere feels seductively unreal. We hear some André 3000 and The Love Below-era experimentation in that willingness to blur soul, theater and dream logic.

But the beauty has a purpose: Lucy represents temptation, the forces trying to lure Kendrick toward a different understanding of success. The lushness is the bait. Like “For Free?,” we are leaving the interlude NR as a standard song and handing out a 10/10 for its concept, sound and sequencing.

09
WHAT SUCCESS DOESN’T TEACH YOU

Momma

9.3/ 10

“Momma” eases into a dusty pocket, with Knxwledge’s feel for off-center rhythm setting a different pace. The groove is understated, almost conversational, allowing Kendrick to unravel ideas rather than hammer them home. It sounds grounded after the dreamscape of “For Sale?”

The message is all about the difference between accumulating knowledge and understanding where you belong. Reconnecting with Africa complicates the narrator’s certainty: he can know plenty about fame and still have more to discover about his identity and heritage. A soulful, introspective track that rewards careful listening. We landed at a strong 9.3/10.

10
COMPTON POLITICS, INDUSTRY POLITICS

Hood Politics

9.8/ 10

This is a record Southern California listeners can feel in their bones. Kendrick shifts from a loose opening into a hard, bouncing instrumental and starts talking the language of neighborhoods, affiliations, reputations and loyalty. The hook is playful, but nothing about the underlying power dynamics is simple.

The genius is how he connects hood politics to the politics of rap — the alliances and betrayals can look familiar even when the stakes and settings change. We hear a straight-up West Coast banger here, not a dry social-science lesson. The beat hits, the bars land and the cultural specificity gives it an extra layer for anyone who knows Los Angeles.

11
THE PRICE OF PRIDE

How Much a Dollar Cost

feat. James Fauntleroy & Ronald Isley
9.8/ 10

A short encounter between Kendrick and a man asking for money becomes an examination of compassion, arrogance and spirituality. The storytelling works because Kendrick initially allows the narrator to sound justified. He’s protective of what he earned. He questions the stranger’s motives. We are invited to sit with that discomfort.

Then the final revelation changes the meaning of the encounter entirely. Ronald Isley’s voice and the restrained, almost sacred feeling of the music give the conclusion extraordinary weight. No giant chorus is needed when the story itself delivers the blow. This is master-level writing — a 9.8 that earns every decimal.

12
MORE THAN SKIN-DEEP

Complexion (A Zulu Love)

feat. Rapsody
8.9/ 10

Kendrick addresses colorism and inherited ideas about beauty over a soulful groove, and Rapsody meets the moment with a verse that expands the conversation rather than repeating his perspective. The softness of the instrumental might make this one easy to overlook the first few times.

We needed repeated listens to appreciate how the theme connects to the wider album. It’s thoughtful, sometimes subtle, and less instantly gripping than the surrounding heavyweight songs — which is why we landed at 8.9/10, not because the message lacks force. Huge respect to Rapsody for bringing such a strong presence to the record.

13
RAGE, IDENTITY & CONTRADICTION 4DK PICK ★

The Blacker the Berry

9.7/ 10

Kendrick attacks this record with ferocity. The drums land like a warning, the vocal performance is relentless and the song refuses to make its confrontations comfortable. This is one of the project’s hardest songs sonically, and one of its most politically charged lyrically.

It speaks directly to racism and the contradictions Kendrick sees around and within himself. That final turn is meant to provoke, not neatly equate different forms of violence or excuse systemic injustice. Following “Complexion,” its anger hits with even greater force. We gave it 9.7 because the writing is powerful and the record is a certified banger.

14
THE HIDDEN WEST COAST GEM 4DK PICK ★

You Ain’t Gotta Lie (Momma Said)

9.4/ 10

Give this one its flowers. The beat has a warm, rolling bounce, Kendrick sounds comfortable in the pocket, and the whole record carries the easygoing feel of great California music. It’s the kind of deep cut that can slip past listeners drawn to the album’s louder, more celebrated moments.

But its advice is essential: stop performing somebody else’s version of yourself. After an album full of temptation, identity crises and the pressure of expectations, that message hits with the wisdom of somebody bringing you back to earth. One of our seven 4DK Picks and a 9.4/10 hidden gem.

15
THE ANSWER TO “u”

i

8.7/ 10

The Isley Brothers foundation gives “i” a celebratory, uplifting spirit. The album version bends that energy into a staged live setting, complete with crowd sounds, interruption and Kendrick stepping forward to address the room. It sounds less like a finished pop single and more like a communal moment unfolding in real time.

As a piece of the album, that choice matters: where “u” tore the narrator apart, “i” reaches for self-love. We appreciate the intention and the musicianship, even if it isn’t one of the tracks we return to most. 8.7/10 for the song; an even bigger place in the album’s architecture.

16
THE ULTIMATE CLOSING ARGUMENT 4DK PICK ★

Mortal Man

10.0/ 10

Twelve minutes, and none of the ambition feels wasted. Over an expansive, soulful arrangement Kendrick asks what loyalty really means when an artist, a leader or a public figure falls short of the image people built around them. He questions fame, responsibility, trust, betrayal and the almost impossible expectations placed on people asked to represent something larger than themselves.

Then the imagined conversation with 2Pac, constructed from archival interview audio, makes the album’s conversation across generations explicit. It’s an audacious creative decision, and it lands because the themes have been building since track one. The poem threading through the record finally comes into view. Our strongest comparison is to Kendrick’s early The Heart Parts 1–3 — except “Mortal Man” feels like the extended, fully realized statement those intimate reflections were leading toward. The song, the sequence, the Pac exchange: 10/10, genius from start to finish.

THE IMPACT · THE INFLUENCE · THE ELEVEN-YEAR TEST

MORE THAN A RAP ALBUM.

In 2015, the easy move was to build another blockbuster. Kendrick made something with more brass, more poetry, more unresolved questions and fewer concessions. That decision mattered. It showed that a mainstream rap superstar did not have to choose between mass attention and complete musical ambition.

Its reception proved the work could connect beyond the audience that expected another collection of Compton stories. “Alright” became a real-world protest anthem. The album later won the Grammy for Best Rap Album, and its presence in the Album of the Year conversation underscored how far this particular sound had traveled.

But awards alone cannot explain the legacy. There is a generation of listeners who discovered jazz players, funk innovators and new kinds of rap arrangements because this album opened the door. There is also a generation of artists who saw that an album could carry politics, spirituality, theater and deep self-examination while still being unmistakably hip-hop.

For us, the enduring achievement is how personal the big ideas remain. Kendrick is not floating above the issues he addresses. He implicates himself. He sounds frightened, arrogant, funny, conflicted, inspired, exhausted and hopeful at different points. The contradictions are not a weakness in the concept — they are the concept.

And the ending makes the influence of 2Pac impossible to separate from Kendrick’s own mission. We hear “Mortal Man” as an expanded cousin to the early The Heart records: that direct, searching form of communication now attached to a sprawling album-long argument about what it means to speak for people without pretending you have every answer.

Eleven years later, To Pimp a Butterfly remains a demanding listen in spots — and a rewarding one for exactly that reason. Not every great album has to meet you halfway. Some ask you to grow into them.

THE FINAL WORD · 4 DA KULTURE

AN UNDENIABLE MASTERPIECE.

Our verdict is 9.8/10. Two flawless records in “These Walls” and “Mortal Man,” an extraordinary run of near-perfect material, two brilliantly placed interludes, and a closing statement that ties the entire journey back to Compton, California and the voice of 2Pac. We gave the album its flowers because it earned them — track by track, idea by idea, all these years later.
