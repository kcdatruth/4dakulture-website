DEADLINE FILE 01 · Jaguars · Eagles · Falcons · Saints

Jacksonville goes all-in: Jordan Davis, Jessie Bates and Davon Godchaux

Reported compensation: Davis: 2027 first + 2029 second. Bates: 2027 second + 2028 fifth. Godchaux: conditional 2028 sixth.

Jacksonville has made the loudest statement of the deadline. Jordan Davis gives the front a massive interior presence who can occupy blockers, compress rushing lanes and help keep linebackers clean. Davon Godchaux adds another experienced interior body, increasing the options for heavy fronts and short-yardage packages. Jessie Bates changes the back end: his anticipation and range allow the defense to disguise coverage and challenge quarterbacks who assume the middle of the field will be available. The additions are complementary, not redundant.

The playoff calculation is immediate. At 6–2, Jacksonville has a lead in the AFC South and is competing for a favorable January path. Against high-end quarterbacks, the ability to make opponents one-dimensional and then rotate coverage behind the rush is a legitimate postseason advantage. The risk is also unusually large: surrendering premium picks for Davis and Bates means this is not a free short-term rental strategy. We see a potential conference-title defense, but the pieces still have to communicate and execute together before we declare Jacksonville the AFC favorite.

The selling teams face different realities. Philadelphia loses a foundational interior defender during a 2–7 season; Atlanta parts with a premier safety while still alive in the weak NFC South; New Orleans trades a veteran tackle for future flexibility. Each move can create snaps for younger players, but it also weakens the current roster in a division race where a single win can still matter.

DEADLINE FILE 02 · 49ers · Colts

San Francisco attacks both sides: Keenan Allen and DeForest Buckner

Reported compensation: Allen: 2027 sixth-round pick. Buckner: 2027 fourth-round pick.

San Francisco acquired DeForest Buckner before the deadline and followed with Keenan Allen. Buckner's value is not limited to sacks: interior disruption forces quarterbacks off their spot and makes outside rushers more dangerous by eliminating easy escape lanes. Allen brings veteran route craft, reliable spacing and a third-down outlet for Brock Purdy. He can work underneath coverage that tilts toward explosive targets, creating a more varied passing menu without requiring every completion to be a deep shot.

The 49ers are 7–1, and the NFC West is theirs to control. These moves are about surviving the postseason's different game scripts. Against a pressure-heavy opponent, Allen can help Purdy get the ball out quickly; against a run-first offense, Buckner can change early downs. Neither acquisition guarantees immediate chemistry, and veteran availability matters. But the combined cost is modest compared with the potential reward. Indianapolis, meanwhile, is moving present-day talent for future selections during a 2–7 start, sacrificing offensive reliability and defensive depth in the short term.

DEADLINE FILE 03 · Vikings · Raiders

Minnesota brings back Kirk Cousins: a quarterback reset under pressure

Reported compensation: Cousins to Minnesota for a 2027 third-round pick.

Minnesota's 6–2 record conceals a significant offensive problem, made visible by the six-point performance against Buffalo. Cousins offers experience in timing-based passing, pre-snap identification and the ability to operate a structured offense from the pocket. His familiarity with the franchise makes this reunion especially notable, but familiarity does not eliminate the need to rebuild timing with the current personnel or to protect a quarterback who is less threatening as a designed runner.

The NFC North implications are enormous. Chicago is 7–2, Detroit is 6–2 and Green Bay is 6–3; Minnesota cannot afford several weeks of offensive instability while that group trades blows. If Cousins steadies the passing game, the Vikings become a more credible division and wild-card threat. If protection or health undermines him, a third-round investment may not deliver the expected return. Las Vegas gets a future asset but removes an experienced passer from a 4–5 roster, making its remaining quarterback plan and competitive ambitions a central question.

DEADLINE FILE 04 · Chiefs · Colts

Kansas City adds Josh Downs: the chain-moving element

Reported compensation: Downs and a 2027 seventh to Kansas City for 2027 fourth- and fifth-round picks.

Josh Downs gives Patrick Mahomes a receiver who can threaten from the slot, uncover quickly against zone and turn third-and-medium into manageable throws. The attraction is role clarity: Kansas City does not need every acquisition to be a featured deep threat. A player who wins with leverage and timing can help sustain drives and punish defenses for loading up against other weapons. How quickly he absorbs the route adjustments and option concepts will determine the immediate payoff.

At 6–2, the Chiefs are already contenders, but Denver's 7–2 record keeps the AFC West competitive. An extra dependable target could matter in one-score games and on playoff possessions where defenses take away the first read. The price is not trivial for a young receiver, yet it is more measured than the Jaguars' draft spending. For Indianapolis, dealing Downs alongside Allen and Buckner makes the direction unmistakable: future flexibility has taken priority over short-term offensive stability.

DEADLINE FILE 05 · Bills · Titans

Buffalo adds Amani Hooker: coverage flexibility at a premium

Reported compensation: Hooker and a fifth-round pick to Buffalo for a second-round pick.

Buffalo's 7–1 record puts the team in position to compete for the AFC's top seed. Amani Hooker gives the secondary another experienced safety who can contribute to split-safety looks, handle underneath responsibilities and help disguise rotations. The value of a versatile safety is especially high in January, when elite quarterbacks test communication rather than simply throwing at obvious mismatches.

The investment is meaningful: a second-round selection is a substantial cost for a defensive addition. Buffalo is betting that reducing explosive plays and creating more flexible coverage packages will matter more than preserving that future pick. The Titans, at 1–7, gain draft capital but lose an established defensive piece. We view this as a move aimed at playoff matchups rather than a reaction to one bad regular-season game.

DEADLINE FILE 06 · Broncos · Chargers

Denver buys linebacker help from a division rival

Reported compensation: Daiyan Henley to Denver for 2027 fourth- and fifth-round picks.

Denver is 7–2, with a five-game winning streak, but the narrow margins in several wins show why defensive depth still matters. Daiyan Henley offers athleticism and pursuit range at linebacker, potentially helping Denver cover space against backs and tight ends while supporting its front against the run. Integration will depend on communication and assignment clarity, especially when the defense uses pressure or disguised looks.

The trade is unusual because the seller is a division rival. Los Angeles is 0–8 and receives draft assets, while Denver adds a player who can help in a playoff race. The Broncos are trying to turn close victories into a sustainable identity; the Chargers are prioritizing the next roster-building cycle. The risk for Denver is paying meaningful mid-round capital without getting a full-time impact player immediately.

DEADLINE FILE 07 · Lions · Jets

Detroit adds Andre Cisco to reinforce the back end

Reported compensation: Cisco to Detroit for a 2028 sixth-round pick.

Detroit is 6–2 and coming off a defensive-minded win over Miami. Andre Cisco offers an experienced safety option who can help with deep-field range, communication and ball tracking. A defense with multiple trustworthy coverage pieces can change how aggressively it calls pressure, because it has fewer obvious weak points for opponents to isolate.

This is a relatively inexpensive way to address a position group before the NFC North stretch run. Detroit does not need Cisco to be the sole reason it wins games; it needs him to make the defense more resilient when opponents spread the field. The Jets, at 2–7, turn a veteran contributor into a future pick but lose immediate secondary depth.

DEADLINE FILE 08 · Bears · Titans

Chicago's Jeffery Simmons gamble: the price of becoming a contender

Reported compensation: Simmons and a 2028 fifth to Chicago for 2027 first, 2027 fourth and 2028 first.

Chicago's acquisition of Jeffery Simmons before the deadline is arguably the most aggressive long-term wager in the market. Simmons can collapse the pocket from inside, command double teams and improve the defense against downhill running attacks. For a 7–2 Bears team, that changes the potential matchup against NFC opponents that rely on protection integrity and early-down efficiency. The front can create more pressure without always having to send extra rushers.

Two first-round selections are a steep price, especially when a young contender still needs affordable talent in future seasons. The Bears are betting that the current window is real and that Simmons can help them win games their offense may not dominate—particularly after a victory in which Caleb Williams threw four interceptions. Tennessee receives major draft capital but parts with a centerpiece. The trade raises Chicago's defensive ceiling while dramatically increasing the cost of failing to make a deep postseason run.

DEADLINE FILE 09 · Ravens · Cardinals

Baltimore adds Jacoby Brissett: an insurance policy with immediate stakes

Reported compensation: Brissett and a 2027 fifth to Baltimore for a 2027 fourth and 2028 seventh.

Baltimore's move for Jacoby Brissett followed injuries to its quarterbacks. Brissett offers veteran experience, an ability to operate a conventional passing structure and a stabilizing presence for a team that cannot afford prolonged offensive uncertainty. He does not replicate Lamar Jackson's running threat or the way that threat changes defensive spacing. Any offensive plan built around him must therefore be adjusted, not simply copied.

The Ravens are 4–5 and falling behind Pittsburgh and Cincinnati. A steady backup can keep the season from slipping away if the starter cannot return promptly, but the trade does not solve broader defensive or protection issues. Arizona gains draft flexibility during a 3–6 season while moving a quarterback who had fallen down its depth chart. This is a survival move, not a declaration that Baltimore has fixed everything.

DEADLINE FILE 10 · Colts · Eagles · Falcons · Saints · Titans · Jets · Cardinals · Raiders

The teams that sold—and the contenders that stood still

Reported compensation: Deadline interpretation · draft capital and roster opportunity cost

The trade deadline is not simply a list of players joining contenders. It also reveals which front offices believe the current season can be saved and which are prioritizing future options. Indianapolis' departures of Buckner, Allen and Downs reduce the team's present-day ceiling but add picks and flexibility. Tennessee's decisions around Simmons and Hooker follow a similar logic at a higher draft-capital level. Philadelphia's Jordan Davis trade is especially consequential because premium interior defenders are difficult to replace quickly.

Not every team that declined to make a splash made a mistake. A deadline trade can cost a valuable pick, consume cap flexibility and require a new player to learn terminology in the middle of the season. The better question is whether a roster's most serious weakness was addressable at a sensible price. We will judge these decisions in January through playing time, schematic fit and playoff results—not by who won the social-media headline on deadline day.
