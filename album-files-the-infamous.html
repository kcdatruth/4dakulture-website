THE
INFAMOUS.

Two young MCs. One legendary sound. The making of a Queensbridge classic.

9.3 / 10 · HIP-HOP CLASSIC
MOBB DEEP · APRIL 25, 1995 · LOUD / RCA · QUEENSBRIDGE, NEW YORK
THE 4DK DEEP DIVE

TWO TWENTY-YEAR-OLDS. AN ENTIRE SOUND.

In 1995, hip-hop’s regional identities were flourishing. Nas, Biggie and Wu-Tang had brought renewed attention to New York; OutKast, UGK and 8Ball & MJG were shaping the South; and Tha Dogg Pound represented a different kind of West Coast chemistry. In the middle of that extraordinary moment, Prodigy and Havoc—both only 20 when this album arrived—delivered one of rap’s most fully realized second albums.

The Infamous sounds like its own environment. There is no attempt to sand away the paranoia, pressure or street-level storytelling. Instead, the duo turn those feelings into an aesthetic: sparse arrangements, unforgettable drums and verses that feel lived in. That maturity, at such a young age, remains staggering.

PRODUCER SPOTLIGHT

HAVOC DESERVES HIS FLOWERS.

When the great producers of the 1990s are discussed, DJ Premier, RZA, Dr. Dre and Pete Rock rightly enter the conversation. Havoc belongs there too. His ominous, economical production gave Mobb Deep a sound that was instantly recognizable. Even better, he was simultaneously delivering standout verses: his work on “Survival of the Fittest” and “Cradle to the Grave” makes the case on its own.

Q-Tip’s production contributions add welcome texture to the album, especially on “Give Up the Goods,” “Temperature’s Rising” and “Drink Away the Pain.” But Havoc’s overall sonic vision is the backbone. His later chemistry with Alchemist never erases how foundational his own production was to the Mobb Deep identity.

THE 1995 CONTEXT

TWO TWENTY-YEAR-OLDS IN A GOLDEN ERA.

When The Infamous arrived, Havoc and Prodigy were both only 20. Their command of mood and narrative would be impressive at any age; against the competition of 1995, it becomes even more striking. New York had Nas, Biggie, and Wu-Tang, while OutKast, UGK, 8Ball & MJG, and Tha Dogg Pound were building distinct regional identities.

Mobb Deep did not win attention by sounding like those peers. They made Queensbridge feel like a place with its own weather: cold drums, narrow spaces, constant pressure, and voices that knew the terrain. That distinctiveness—not just the fame of Shook Ones—is the album’s enduring achievement.

01. The Start of Your Ending (41st Side)

9.5 / 10

The title announces the album’s worldview before either MC finishes a verse. Havoc and Prodigy establish a raw mid-’90s East Coast atmosphere immediately: an introduction that sounds like Queensbridge and promises no compromises.

The title is a threat and a thesis at once. Before the album has made its case, the listener already knows there will be no glossy escape hatch. The track’s controlled menace comes from how Prodigy and Havoc refuse to overexplain their world; the setting emerges through cadence, detail, and the way each voice occupies the beat.

As an opening cut, it does something more valuable than simply announcing that the duo can rap: it teaches us how to hear the rest of the album. That straight mid-’90s East Coast texture and the interplay between both MCs make 9.5 feel earned. The song title is part of the performance.

02. The Infamous Prelude

INTERLUDE · NOT RATED

A brief interlude that deepens the album’s street-level atmosphere.

This spoken passage is not being treated as a conventional song. Its purpose is pacing: the record wants to feel like a sequence of scenes, not a shuffled collection of singles.

The interruption also makes the transition into “Survival of the Fittest” more dramatic. Keeping the prelude in its original position matters to the album’s architecture, even though it receives no numerical score.

03. Survival of the Fittest

9.8 / 10

One of Mobb Deep’s defining classics, powered by a haunting Havoc instrumental that belongs in any conversation about hip-hop’s greatest beats. Prodigy commands attention, but Havoc’s fiery verse is essential to the record’s force.

The beat works through restraint. Its eerie keyboard figure, bass weight, and hard-edged percussion leave open space for the rappers’ voices, and that negative space is a huge part of its power. Havoc makes a relatively spare arrangement feel enormous.

Prodigy brings the record’s survival philosophy into sharp focus, but Havoc is no passenger: his verse has the bite and conviction to stand beside his partner’s. That is the point of the 9.8: an all-time instrumental married to genuine two-MC chemistry, not just a famous hook or a single standout performance.

04. Eye for a Eye (Your Beef Is Mines)

10.0 / 10

Nas and Raekwon complement Mobb Deep perfectly. Four distinct voices share one grimy East Coast canvas without diluting the duo’s identity. The chemistry, menace and vivid writing make this a flawless collaboration.

Nas and Raekwon do not sound like celebrity guests parachuting into a single. Nas brings his intricate Queensbridge perspective; Rae’s compact street imagery adds another angle to the same threatening landscape. Their differences make the song richer rather than pulling it apart.

The remarkable achievement is that the hosts retain ownership of the track. Prodigy and Havoc meet the guests on equal ground, and Havoc’s backdrop makes all four voices feel as though they inhabit the same scene. It is the kind of collaboration that explains the creative density of New York rap in 1995.

05. Just Step Prelude

INTERLUDE · NOT RATED

A short connective piece that keeps the original album sequence intact.

A short connective scene, rather than a song to be graded. Its placement is part of the record’s rhythm: dialogue and atmosphere lead directly into the next musical chapter.

The prelude earns its space by reinforcing the feeling that we are listening to an environment with people and movement beyond the verses. It stays unrated, but it is not disposable.

06. Give Up the Goods (Just Step)

9.2 / 10

Big Noyd brings unmistakable Queensbridge energy to this Q-Tip-produced standout. The piano-led groove gives the record bounce without compromising its street character; another reminder of the album’s range.

The piano figure supplies a bounce that contrasts with the oppressive mood of several surrounding songs. Q-Tip’s touch widens the sonic palette without making Mobb Deep sound like a different group. The track feels social and kinetic, even while its language stays tough.

Big Noyd contributes more than a guest cameo: his presence makes the record sound like a Queensbridge crew statement. The 9.2 recognizes a beat that sticks and performances that move, while reserving the very top tier for the album’s most overwhelming moments.

07. Temperature’s Rising

9.5 / 10

A vivid account of loyalty and legal pressure, framed by Crystal Johnson’s soulful hook. The warmth of the production and singing only sharpens the gravity of the story.

This is one of the record’s strongest examples of narrative discipline. The subject is not abstract toughness but the consequences of pressure on people and relationships. Prodigy and Havoc communicate urgency without turning the story into a lecture.

Crystal Johnson’s singing softens the surface but not the stakes. That contrast is the song’s emotional engine: the music invites you closer while the lyrics make the situation feel increasingly precarious. Its 9.5 comes from how fully the arrangement serves the story.

08. Up North Trip

8.8 / 10

The duo examine incarceration and survival with the same cold-eyed perspective that anchors the album. A strong cut that contributes to its larger portrait, even if it falls below the highest peaks.

Where the album’s most celebrated songs sound like warnings delivered in the street, this one looks at what happens after the consequences arrive. The title alone signals incarceration and distance, and the duo’s plainspoken delivery makes the situation feel ordinary in the most unsettling way.

Its value is cumulative. It expands the album’s world beyond confrontation to confinement and endurance, connecting the record’s threats to their aftermath. An 8.8 is still a strong endorsement, even if the song does not have the immediate musical grip of the highest-rated cuts.

09. Trife Life

9.8 / 10

Our hidden gem. Prodigy’s storytelling is gripping, and the smooth, understated beat gives the narrative space to unfold. Its calm delivery makes the tension hit harder: storytelling rap at its finest.

This is the hidden gem that deserves a much louder conversation. Prodigy tells the story with such control that the tension builds almost quietly. He trusts the details and the sequence of events rather than reaching for a dramatic vocal effect at every turn.

The beat is smooth and hypnotic, an especially smart choice for a track driven by narrative. Instead of announcing danger with every drum hit, the production lets it creep into the room. That marriage of restraint and vivid storytelling is why this one sits at 9.8.

10. Q.U. — Hectic

8.7 / 10

Rugged Queensbridge energy, aggressive performances and a sound that refuses to soften the album’s identity. A solid deep cut.

The track pulls the listener back toward the roughest edges of the group’s identity. Its aggression is not decorative; it reinforces the album’s constant sense of vigilance, and both MCs sound comfortable in that pressure.

What keeps it at 8.7 rather than in the 9.5-plus tier is the absence of one singular element—a verse, narrative turn, or instrumental idea—that changes the temperature of the whole record. It is effective sequencing and strong atmosphere, even without being a centerpiece.

11. Right Back at You

8.4 / 10

Ghostface Killah and Raekwon deliver fiery guest verses alongside Big Noyd. The lyricism stands tall, but the instrumental is less compelling than the album’s most memorable production.

Ghostface and Raekwon inject personality and urgency, while Big Noyd helps keep the record anchored in Mobb Deep’s immediate circle. There is plenty to admire in the guest performances: distinct voices with no loss of intensity.

The reservation is the instrumental. Compared with the haunting elegance of “Survival of the Fittest” or the smooth tension of “Trife Life,” this beat leaves less of an impression. An 8.4 is not a dismissal of the rapping; it is a reminder that production matters just as much to our final judgment.

12. The Grave Prelude

INTERLUDE · NOT RATED

The last interlude provides a transition into the record’s closing run.

The final prelude functions like a scene change before the album’s last major run. It creates a pause that makes “Cradle to the Grave” feel less like another track and more like the start of a concluding statement.

That structural purpose is why the three interludes remain part of the full review. They are not rated as songs, but the album’s atmosphere would be different without their pacing.

13. Cradle to the Grave

10.0 / 10

A personal favorite and a storytelling masterpiece. Havoc kills his verse; Prodigy’s final two verses are extraordinary. The simple, effective instrumental complements the narrative instead of fighting for attention.

Havoc’s verse is a major part of this song’s greatness. Too often his contributions are summarized as “the beats,” but here his voice, timing, and conviction are integral to the record. He sounds like an equal storyteller, not an artist filling space before Prodigy returns.

Prodigy’s final two verses push the narrative into another class. The instrumental is simple but exactly right: nothing distracts from the writing, and the beat gives the story room to breathe. This is one of our personal Mobb Deep favorites, and a full 10 because every choice supports the storytelling.

14. Drink Away the Pain (Situations)

9.0 / 10

Q-Tip’s presence and production introduce a warmer sound, while imaginative brand-name metaphors explore relationships and personal strain. A creative change of pace that still fits the project.

The change in texture is immediate. Q-Tip’s production gives the song a warmer, more reflective atmosphere, proving that this album’s sonic identity can bend without breaking. It is a useful release of pressure after so much darkness.

The writing is clever in a way that rewards attention: brands become shorthand for relationships, temptation, and emotional complication. The concept could have been a novelty, but the performances keep it grounded. A 9.0 reflects both its inventiveness and how naturally it fits into the album.

15. Shook Ones, Pt. II

10.0 / 10

The record that helped make Mobb Deep a household name in hip-hop. Prodigy’s iconic verse, Havoc’s grimy production and the unforgettable video imagery—including the banging pots—form an enduring cultural landmark.

Havoc’s production is a case study in turning a few perfectly chosen elements into a whole atmosphere. The beat is instantly recognizable, but its real achievement is the tension it sustains under every line. Nothing sounds wasted.

Prodigy’s opening verse is part of hip-hop’s shared memory, yet the record is bigger than one performance. Havoc’s contribution, the chemistry, and the gritty video imagery—including the pots being struck in the background—made the song feel inseparable from its neighborhood. It helped bring Mobb Deep to a much wider audience without sanding down their sound. A definitive 10.

16. Party Over

8.7 / 10

Big Noyd returns for a raw, fitting finale. The album closes on its own terms: uncompromising and unmistakably Queensbridge.

The closer does not try to manufacture a sentimental ending. Big Noyd’s return reinforces the collective Queensbridge identity that has run through the project, and the energy stays rugged to the last stretch.

That refusal to soften the landing is part of the album’s coherence. “Party Over” is not one of our top-scoring tracks, but its 8.7 reflects a strong finale that sounds like it belongs to the same world as the opening cut.

THE FINAL 4DK VERDICT

AN UNDISPUTED QUEENSBRIDGE CLASSIC.

9.3 / 10

Three perfect 10s—“Eye for a Eye,” “Cradle to the Grave” and “Shook Ones, Pt. II”—tell only part of the story. “Trife Life” is the hidden gem, Havoc’s production is the album’s backbone, and Prodigy’s storytelling elevates even the quietest arrangements. The record is not flawless from start to finish, but its best moments helped define an era.

4DK standouts: Best song — “Shook Ones, Pt. II” · Best beat — “Survival of the Fittest” · Best storytelling — “Cradle to the Grave” · Hidden gem — “Trife Life” · Best collaboration — “Eye for a Eye.”

Final rating is the rounded average of 13 scored songs; the three interludes remain unrated.
