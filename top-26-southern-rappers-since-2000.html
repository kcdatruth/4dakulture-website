THE BEST SOUTHERN RAPPERS
SINCE 2000.

The South didn't just take over rap. For long stretches, the South was rap.

By Kcdatruth • 4 Da Kulture • September 7, 2026
NEW ORLEANSATLANTAHOUSTONMEMPHISBATON ROUGEMIAMIMISSISSIPPICAROLINA

There was a time when Southern rap was treated like the outsider.

Then the 2000s happened.

Atlanta became damn near the center of hip-hop. New Orleans gave us one of the greatest rappers ever. Houston, Memphis, Baton Rouge, Miami, Mississippi and the Carolinas all built their own sounds, movements and superstars.

Crunk. Trap. Trill. Snap. Mixtape rap. Melodic rap.

A lot of what hip-hop sounds like today came through the South first.

THE RULE

If you made music after 2000, you were eligible. But we're judging what you gave us from 2000 forward — catalog, bars, influence, longevity, peak, cultural impact and how much the music still matters.

Groups aren't eligible, so no Migos. And for this series, we're counting Virginia with the East Coast/Mid-Atlantic, not the South.

Now let's argue.

26

Webbie

Webbie represents an era of Louisiana rap that deserves more respect nationally. That Baton Rouge sound was raw, unfiltered and regional in the best possible way. He may not have the catalog of the names above him, but his place in that Trill movement earns him a spot.

25

Trick Daddy

Miami rap existed long before Trick Daddy, but Trick helped give the city a voice that felt completely different from everybody else. Aggressive. Emotional. Street. He was already established before 2000, which hurts him under this specific formula, but his work afterward still matters enough to make the list.

24

Devin the Dude

Devin is one of hip-hop's ultimate cult favorites. He never needed superstar numbers. The laid-back delivery, humor and weeded-out Houston perspective created a lane that was completely his. You know a Devin record when you hear one.

23

DaBaby

People can argue about everything that happened afterward, but DaBaby's peak was real. For a few years, he was everywhere. Hits. Features. Albums. Videos. His run wasn't long enough to push him higher, but at his hottest, DaBaby was legitimately one of rap's biggest stars.

22

Yo Gotti

Gotti's longevity is what separates him. Mixtapes. Memphis street records. Mainstream success. Then he turned CMG into its own movement. He went from rapper to executive without ever completely disappearing as an artist. That deserves respect.

21

Big Boi

And before anybody starts screaming — remember the rules. If this were an all-time Southern rap list, Big Boi is much higher. This ranking is heavily about post-2000 individual work, and André and Big Boi don't automatically get every OutKast accomplishment counted as solo résumé. Still, Big Boi is one of the most technically consistent rappers the South ever produced.

20

Mo3

Mo3 had something you can't teach. Pain. You could hear it in his voice. He could sing, rap, make street records and give you something emotional without sounding like he was forcing any of it. His career ended before we got to see how far he could really go.

19

Juicy J

Juicy J basically had two careers. First, a Southern rap legend. Then somehow he came back and became relevant to an entirely new generation. That's hard as hell to do. The longevity, production instincts and ability to understand where rap was going keep him on this list.

18

Bun B

Bun B is another guy who would look completely different on an all-time Southern list. UGK's history speaks for itself. But even after 2000, Bun remained one of the most respected voices in Southern hip-hop. The bars never left. Neither did the respect.

17

Big K.R.I.T.

K.R.I.T. came along when Southern rap was already dominating and somehow still made Mississippi feel like its own world. He could rap. He could produce. He understood Southern rap history without sounding trapped inside it. K.R.I.T. should've been bigger commercially, but anybody who really followed that mixtape run knows what time it was.

16

Young Dolph

Dolph built his career his way. Independent mindset. Memphis sound. Street records. Business. Consistency. He didn't need to become something else to reach people outside Memphis. The further we get from his career, the more obvious his impact becomes.

15

Kevin Gates

Gates is one of those rappers who can sound dangerous, vulnerable, hilarious and completely unhinged within the same five minutes. And somehow it works. He turned pain, relationships, jail, spirituality, street life and self-help into his own strange universe. Nobody sounds like Gates.

14

Boosie

Boosie's impact goes deeper than Billboard numbers. Especially in Louisiana and across the South. There's a generation of people who don't just know Boosie songs — those records are attached to memories. His music felt like neighborhood reporting. The voice, personality and honesty made him impossible to replace.

13

2 Chainz

2 Chainz reinvented himself in public. That alone is impressive. He went from being known as Tity Boi to becoming one of the most reliable rappers of the 2010s. Features. Punchlines. Hits. Albums. And somehow he made ridiculous bars sound cool enough that nobody questioned them 😂.

12

Scarface

Same disclaimer as Big Boi and Bun. All-time Scarface? He's way higher. But this is post-2000. Even under that restriction, Face still belongs. Few rappers ever wrote about depression, mortality, violence and consequences with his level of gravity. When Scarface talks, you listen.

11

Lil Baby

Lil Baby went from sounding like another promising Atlanta rapper to becoming one of the defining voices of his generation. The growth happened fast. His flow got sharper. His writing improved. His records got bigger. And for a stretch, it felt like every important rap album needed a Lil Baby verse. He still has time to climb even higher.

10

Gucci Mane

You cannot tell the story of modern Atlanta rap without Gucci. Period. The mixtapes alone changed everything. Then look at how many rappers came through his orbit. Future. Young Thug. Migos. Waka. And countless others. Gucci's influence is bigger than his own catalog, and his catalog is already massive.

TOP NINETHE SOUTH GOT HEAVY
9

Young Thug

Young Thug changed what a rapper was allowed to sound like. People laughed at him early. Then five years later, half the game sounded like him. The voices. Melodies. Cadences. Fashion. Song structures. Thug didn't just make records. He opened doors.

8

Rick Ross

Ross built one of rap's most recognizable worlds. Maybachs. Luxury. Drug-kingpin imagery. Soul samples. Big production. That voice. And the catalog is deeper than people sometimes give him credit for. He might not be the most technically gifted rapper in the top 10, but he understands how to make a record sound important.

7

Future

Putting Future at seven is probably going to piss somebody off 😂. That's fine. His influence on modern rap is enormous. Melody. Drugged-out vulnerability. Toxic relationship records. Trap production. Flows that entire generations borrowed. Future could easily be higher on somebody else's list. But seven on this list still puts him among the absolute giants.

6

Ludacris

People forget how dominant Luda was. He had hits. He had albums. He had videos everybody remembered. And most importantly, he could rap his ass off. Funny bars. Technical verses. Crazy flows. Battle energy. Ludacris became so successful outside music that people started underrating the rapper. Don't do that.

THE TOP FIVEKINGS OF THE SOUTH
5

J. Cole

Cole is North Carolina — and that's the South all day. And from a pure rapper standpoint, he belongs this high. He built a career without needing gimmicks. Albums. Storytelling. Production. Features. A loyal fanbase that grew with him. Cole's biggest strength might be that people actually care what he has to say. That matters.

4

André 3000

André is complicated on a list like this because he barely gave us traditional solo rap albums. But the verses? Come on. Even after OutKast slowed down, one André feature could become an event. He could disappear for two years, show up for sixteen bars and remind everybody why he's in all-time conversations. The limited solo catalog keeps him from the top three. The talent keeps him at four.

3

Jeezy

Jeezy didn't invent trap music. But he helped turn it into a national language. When Thug Motivation 101 hit, the energy shifted. The ad-libs. The beats. The voice. The motivational street talk. Jeezy made hustling sound like a corporate mission statement 😂. And beyond the peak, he built a catalog that lasted. For a certain generation, Jeezy wasn't just a rapper. He was the soundtrack.

2

T.I.

T.I. called himself the King of the South before everybody agreed. Eventually, people stopped laughing. Trap Muzik. Urban Legend. King. T.I. vs. T.I.P. Paper Trail. Street records, radio records, storytelling, technical rapping — Tip could do all of it. And he helped define what Atlanta rap looked and sounded like before Atlanta completely took over hip-hop. If Wayne didn't exist, T.I. would have a serious case for #1.

1
4DK #1

Lil Wayne

Yeah. This one wasn't really that hard. Wayne's 2000s run is one of the greatest stretches any rapper has ever had. Albums. Mixtapes. Features. Freestyles. Leaks. Bars. Punchlines. Experiments. And at his peak, Wayne genuinely felt unavoidable. Tha Carter. Tha Carter II. Da Drought 3. Dedication 2. Tha Carter III. And that's not even touching everything. Wayne influenced how rappers dressed, wrote, recorded, released music and approached mixtapes. He didn't just become the biggest rapper from New Orleans. For a period, he became the biggest rapper period. When we're talking about Southern rappers from 2000 forward — Lil Wayne is #1.

26 rappers. Atlanta. New Orleans. Houston. Memphis. Baton Rouge. Miami. Mississippi. Carolina.

The South didn't just get its turn.
The South changed what hip-hop sounded like.
