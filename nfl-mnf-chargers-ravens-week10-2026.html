THE WINLESS CHARGERS FOUND THEIR EDGE

Los Angeles arrived in Baltimore carrying an 0–8 record and an 11-game losing streak stretching into last season. Baltimore, once discussed as a championship contender, entered with its own four-game skid and a quarterback room ravaged by injuries. This was a matchup of desperate teams, but the Chargers played like the team that understood precisely what desperation demanded: win the line of scrimmage, run with conviction and make Baltimore earn every yard. The final score was only 23–20. The difference in physical authority was much wider.

160–16: THE NUMBER THAT EXPLAINS EVERYTHING

The Chargers outrushed Baltimore 160 yards to 16. Derrick Henry, one of football's defining power backs, finished with 10 yards on 10 carries. The Ravens had zero rushing yards at halftime. That is not simply a bad night for one running back; it is an indictment of the blocking, play sequencing and ability to dictate terms. When Baltimore needed its run game to protect a new starting quarterback, it instead handed Jacoby Brissett the responsibility of carrying the offense through the air. Los Angeles won the matchup in the trenches before the final turnover made it official.

HERBERT'S FIRST PUNCH

Justin Herbert threw for only 129 yards on 12 completions, but his opening drive established the Chargers' intent. His 22-yard touchdown run put Los Angeles ahead 7–0 and forced Baltimore to chase the game from the beginning. Herbert also added 51 rushing yards. The performance wasn't built around a spectacular passing total. It was about taking what was available, keeping the offense moving and resisting the temptation to turn every snap into a rescue mission. The Chargers had been searching for a workable offensive identity all season; for one night, it was physical, efficient and uncomplicated.

HAMPTON'S FOURTH-DOWN STATEMENT

Omarion Hampton's 22 carries, 105 yards and touchdown were the backbone of the win. His defining play came late in the second quarter, after Baltimore's offense surrendered an interception and Los Angeles aggressively stayed on the field on fourth down. On fourth-and-goal from the 1, Hampton fought through contact to score, pushing the lead to 17–7. It was a call that could have backfired on a winless team desperate to avoid another mistake. Instead, Jim Harbaugh trusted his line and his runner. That trust paid off, and Hampton continued to wear Baltimore down after halftime.

TRE HARRIS GAVE THE OFFENSE ITS EARLY SPARK

The rushing attack rightly owns the headline, but Tre Harris helped create the early cushion. He caught five passes for 86 yards, all before halftime, including gains that set up Herbert's opening touchdown and a Cameron Dicker field goal. Baltimore's defense could not afford to concede explosive early plays while its offense kept going three-and-out. Harris made the Ravens respect the perimeter long enough for Hampton and Herbert to control the ground game.

BRISSETT COMPETED; BALTIMORE'S IDENTITY DIDN'T

Jacoby Brissett completed 33 of 49 passes for 340 yards with one interception in his Ravens debut. Those numbers deserve context. With Lamar Jackson and Tyler Huntley on injured reserve, Baltimore asked a recently acquired quarterback to solve a season's worth of problems on short notice. Brissett eventually moved the ball, with Mark Andrews contributing eight catches for 82 yards, but the Ravens opened with three consecutive three-and-outs and spent much of the night playing catch-up. Derrick Henry's two short touchdown runs kept them alive on the scoreboard. They could not hide the larger failure: an offense built to impose itself physically was reduced to a one-dimensional chase.

THE PLAY THAT CLOSED THE DOOR

Baltimore clawed within 23–20 after Henry's one-yard touchdown in the fourth quarter. With just over two minutes left, the Ravens had a chance to tie or steal the game. Brissett found Zay Flowers for a completion near midfield, but Tarheeb Still kept fighting through the catch, forced the ball loose and Elijah Molden recovered. The turnover was decisive. Herbert later connected with former Raven Charlie Kolar for a critical first down, allowing Los Angeles to finish the job. For a team that had spent months finding ways to lose, this time the Chargers made the winning play.

INJURIES: THE CONTEXT, NOT THE EXCUSE

Baltimore played without Lamar Jackson and Tyler Huntley, both on injured reserve. During the game, guard Vega Ioane left with a shoulder injury and defensive tackle Nnamdi Madubuike with a hamstring injury. Chargers cornerback Rodney Shelley exited with an ankle injury. The long-term severity of those in-game injuries was not confirmed in the immediate postgame reporting. Injuries help explain why Baltimore's margin for error has shrunk, but they do not explain away a 160–16 rushing disparity or a late fumble.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE CHARGERS

Los Angeles is 1–8, and one victory does not magically restore a lost season. But it does change the questions worth asking. Herbert showed he can lead a patient offense rather than chase hero plays; Hampton showed he can be the unquestioned lead back; the defense proved it can close a tight game. The Chargers should build on those habits, especially with the Jets next. The record remains bleak, but the tape finally contains a foundation.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE RAVENS

Baltimore falls to 4–6 with five consecutive losses. The playoff chase is no longer a distant abstraction; it is an immediate emergency. Brissett offered enough passing production to keep the game alive, but the complete disappearance of the rushing attack is the bigger warning. Baltimore cannot ask its replacement quarterback to throw nearly 50 times while Henry gets nothing behind the line. The Ravens need health, but more urgently they need an offensive identity and a way to stop giving opponents the physical advantage.

THE 4DK VERDICT

The Chargers didn't steal Monday night. They earned it in the hardest part of the sport: the trenches. Hampton ran with authority, Herbert supplied the opening statement and the defense delivered the closing play. Baltimore's problem is bigger than a backup quarterback or a single fumble. A team with championship expectations has stopped looking like itself. Los Angeles finally gave its locker room a reason to believe. Baltimore must decide whether it can still become the team it expected to be before the season runs out.
