4DK FILM ROOM · CHAPTER 01

The second act is not nostalgia

Golden State's early season is a study in how a dynasty evolves without surrendering its competitive instincts.

An 0–3 start would have been easy to frame as the beginning of the end. Instead, Golden State won eight straight games and reached 8–3 before a 115–108 defeat in Houston on November 11 dropped the Warriors to 8–4. The streak mattered because it demonstrated that this team has more than one way to manufacture offense and sustain effort. It also raised a more difficult question: how much of that improvement survives when Stephen Curry is unavailable?

4DK's projection is deliberately measured: 46–49 wins, with a sixth- or seventh-seed finish as the central expectation. That is not a championship prediction, and it is not a dismissal of the Warriors' upside. A healthy Curry remains the sort of singular offensive force who changes a playoff opponent's entire coverage. A developing Yaxel Lendeborg and an ascending Brandin Podziemski make the surrounding roster more interesting than a simple aging-core narrative suggests.

This is the central tension of Issue 04. The Warriors are trying to preserve a championship window while discovering which players might keep the franchise competitive after that window closes. The same season can be both a last great push and the beginning of something new.

4DK FILM ROOM · CHAPTER 02

Steph Curry: the ceiling and the emergency

Even when the Warriors find new scorers, Curry remains the player who bends the floor.

Curry's value cannot be reduced to points per game. His movement changes the geometry of possessions. Defenders follow him away from the ball, switching assignments early and opening passing lanes that disappear when he sits. That gravity creates simpler decisions for young teammates. It is why the Warriors can look unusually connected when he is on the floor and considerably more ordinary when opponents no longer have to chase him through screens.

The injury now makes that contrast unavoidable. Golden State confirmed on November 12 that an MRI revealed an oblique abdominal tear after Curry exited the Houston game in the first quarter. The team said he would be reevaluated in two weeks. Reevaluation is not a guaranteed return date. The Warriors must treat this as a real absence, not a short timeout before the same offense resumes.

There is a silver lining only if the organization handles the stretch correctly. The staff can find out who initiates offense when the most feared shooter in basketball is unavailable. It can measure whether the supporting cast creates efficient attempts without a defense already distorted by Curry. And it can protect the veteran who gives this roster its greatest postseason ceiling. A playoff push is worthless if the team spends November rushing its best player back.

4DK FILM ROOM · CHAPTER 03

Yaxel Lendeborg: the bridge that can score

A 24-year-old rookie is giving Golden State both immediate production and a plausible future centerpiece.

Lendeborg is not a traditional teenage prospect whose entire value depends on what he might become in four years. At 24, he arrives with a more developed sense of pace, contact and responsibility. Through 11 games, the NBA's November 11 Rookie Ladder listed him at 21.4 points, 7.2 rebounds and 4.0 assists per game. Those are remarkable early returns, but they are still an 11-game sample rather than proof of a finished All-Star.

What stands out on film is the breadth of the toolkit. Lendeborg can attack a closeout, score through contact, make a passing read and compete defensively. He is willing to take responsibility, and the edge in his personality is part of the package: he does not appear interested in behaving like a quiet passenger simply because Curry is in the building. That confidence is valuable so long as it remains attached to good decisions and consistent defensive work.

Houston supplied the clearest early stress test. After Curry left, Lendeborg scored 25 points and grabbed 10 rebounds. He helped Golden State recover from a 15-point first-quarter deficit and made the Rockets defend his own offense rather than merely the space Curry normally creates. Yet the Warriors still lost. That distinction matters: individual emergence can be real even when the team cannot finish the job.

A sustainable 20-point scorer is a reasonable 4DK developmental target, not a promised season average. Opponents will study his preferred drives, challenge his outside shot and force him to make reads as a primary focus. If he continues scoring while improving his playmaking under those conditions, the franchise will have found something bigger than a complementary rookie. It will have found a potential bridge between eras.

4DK FILM ROOM · CHAPTER 04

Brandin Podziemski: the third option must be earned

The Warriors' offensive hierarchy looks healthier when Podziemski is empowered to make plays.

Golden State's past problems have often surfaced when Curry sat and possessions devolved into difficult, late-clock attempts. Podziemski's emergence offers a different possibility. He can shoot, connect passes, work off the ball and create enough of his own offense to prevent every secondary possession from becoming a scramble. His growth is not only about his scoring average; it is about whether he can reliably identify the right kind of shot.

A dependable third option does not need to dominate the ball. On a roster with Curry and Lendeborg, the most valuable third scorer is often the player who attacks an advantage before the defense can reset. Podziemski has shown signs of becoming that player. The next step is consistency against physical defenders and in games where his three-point shot does not fall.

The Rockets loss illustrated both his importance and the limits of the current group. With Curry gone, Golden State needed another source of offense alongside Lendeborg. The team kept competing but could not close the final gap. The long-term question is whether Podziemski can provide that support night after night without needing Curry to manufacture his first advantage.

4DK FILM ROOM · CHAPTER 05

Draymond Green: change the workload, not the respect

The value of an intelligent veteran is real. So is the cost of playing long stretches with a limited scoring threat.

Draymond remains one of Golden State's best communicators. He organizes switches, recognizes opponent actions before they fully develop and understands the timing of the Warriors' offense. Those traits do not disappear because his scoring output declines. Against Houston he produced seven assists, an example of how he can still influence possessions without carrying the scoring load.

But the offense must also account for what opponents are willing to concede. When a defender can sag away from Green, Curry's driving lanes shrink, Lendeborg sees extra bodies and the team has to work harder for the same quality of attempt. The problem becomes more severe when Curry is absent. Golden State cannot afford too many possessions in which a non-shooting alignment allows opponents to crowd its younger creators.

The 4DK proposal is not to exile a franchise cornerstone. It is to reconsider whether a shorter, targeted role—potentially 18 to 24 minutes in some matchups—could maximize his communication and defensive intelligence while improving offensive spacing. A bench role is a tactical option, not a verdict on his career. The best answer may change depending on opponent, health and which scorers share the floor with him.

4DK FILM ROOM · CHAPTER 06

The bench: small advantages become large

De'Anthony Melton, Brandon Williams and the reserve rotation matter because contenders survive the minutes their stars cannot play.

The bench should be evaluated by what it actually solves. Does it maintain ball pressure? Does it generate enough scoring to avoid a prolonged drought? Can it survive a stretch without Curry or Lendeborg? Melton's willingness to defend and make a timely perimeter shot offers the sort of two-way contribution that complements a high-usage creator. Williams has shown useful scoring ability, but his hamstring issue complicates the short-term rotation.

Depth is not a collection of individual box scores. It is the ability to assemble five players whose strengths cover one another's weaknesses. A reserve unit with multiple willing defenders can still lose if no one initiates a credible action. A lineup full of scorers can still surrender its lead if it cannot finish a possession with a rebound. Golden State must solve those combinations now, while the stakes are still measured in regular-season possessions.

The absence of several rotation players makes it especially important not to treat every early-November lineup as a final judgment. Availability is part of the scouting report. So is the coaching staff's ability to simplify roles when the roster is stretched thin.

4DK FILM ROOM · CHAPTER 07

Jimmy Butler: reintegration before reputation

The correct comeback plan is not to restore an old shot hierarchy on day one.

Butler's value has never been limited to the number of field-goal attempts he takes. At his best he controls tempo, identifies mismatches, draws contact, creates for teammates and brings an edge to defensive possessions. That profile could fit beautifully beside Curry, Lendeborg and Podziemski, provided Golden State does not force every returning player into a preassigned scoring rank.

Butler's ACL recovery must dictate the schedule. When he is medically cleared, the team should rebuild his conditioning, movement confidence and chemistry in stages. There is no reason to rush him into a major offensive burden just to establish who is the second or third option. The players who have earned touches during his absence should not suddenly be asked to stand in the corners.

A healthy Butler could eventually become the lineup's defensive organizer, secondary creator and late-game decision-maker. That does not mean he has to be a passive fourth option, either. It means the Warriors should let the best version of their offense reveal the right hierarchy rather than impose one in advance. The goal is to arrive in spring with several players capable of winning a possession in different ways.

4DK FILM ROOM · CHAPTER 08

The Houston test: effort without the closer

November 11 revealed the promise of the new generation and the fragility of the current roster.

Houston's 115–108 victory ended Golden State's eight-game winning streak. Curry exited after roughly six minutes, and the Rockets built a 35–20 lead after one quarter. The Warriors did not fold. Lendeborg's 25-point, 10-rebound performance powered a comeback, and Golden State repeatedly forced Houston to respond. The game was not a moral victory—there are no standings points for resilience—but it was useful evidence of how the young core reacts when the game plan disappears.

The most revealing sequence came late. Golden State cut the margin to three, but Houston answered with a decisive run, including timely perimeter shooting. Kevin Durant scored 20 for the Rockets, while Alperen Şengün contributed 19 points, 13 rebounds and nine assists. The Warriors generated enough resistance to make the result competitive; they did not generate enough late-game control to finish it.

That is the difference between having exciting individual scorers and having a reliable closing system. Curry normally provides one solution because defenses fear his pull-up range from almost anywhere. Without him, Golden State needs a hierarchy of actions: who starts the possession, where Lendeborg catches the ball, how Podziemski attacks the second side, and which lineup provides the spacing to keep the paint available. Houston exposed a problem worth solving before the postseason.

4DK FILM ROOM · CHAPTER 09

The trade deadline: protect the future

The Warriors should be aggressive about fit and disciplined about what they refuse to sacrifice.

The temptation is obvious. With Curry still capable of changing a playoff series, Golden State could convince itself that every young player is merely a trade chip in the pursuit of one more veteran. But Lendeborg's emergence and Podziemski's development change the calculation. Both players can contribute to the current team while giving the organization a future that does not depend entirely on Curry's health.

A smart deadline approach begins with needs, not names. The Warriors should examine perimeter size, reliable shooting, frontcourt mobility and whether the second unit has enough creation. They should also be honest about the return timelines of Butler and the other injured players. Adding a recognizable scorer who duplicates existing roles may solve less than adding a defender who can shoot and stay on the floor in a playoff series.

Our recommendation is to keep the young core unless an extraordinary, clearly justified opportunity emerges. The franchise cannot afford to confuse urgency with panic. A second act only becomes meaningful if the team avoids trading away the very players who are making it possible.

4DK FILM ROOM · CHAPTER 10

The 4DK verdict: dangerous, not crowned

A sixth- or seventh-seed forecast can coexist with a much higher postseason ceiling.

The base case is 46–49 wins and a finish around sixth or seventh in the West. That forecast recognizes a crowded conference, the fragility created by injuries and the difficulty of sustaining an eight-game winning streak over six months. It also respects the fact that a healthy Curry can make a first-round opponent deeply uncomfortable regardless of the number beside Golden State's name.

The upside case is a Western Conference Finals run. For that to become more than a headline, several things must align: Curry returns healthy; Lendeborg remains effective once defenses prioritize him; Podziemski becomes a trustworthy third creator; Green's role improves the lineups around him; Butler returns without being rushed; and the reserve units survive difficult minutes. None of those developments should be treated as guaranteed.

The bigger story is what Golden State has found while searching for answers. Lendeborg might be a legitimate second scorer and the franchise's next foundation piece. Podziemski might be growing into a reliable offensive partner. Curry remains the reason the ceiling is so high. Butler and Green could provide veteran intelligence without requiring the entire offense to revolve around their reputations.

The Warriors may no longer be the dynasty that once terrified the league, but their future does not have to begin after Curry. It can begin beside him. The question is no longer simply whether Golden State has one more run left. It is whether the organization can build a team that makes that run possible without sacrificing the next one.
