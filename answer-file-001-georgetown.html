GEORGETOWN:
BEFORE HE WAS
THE ANSWER.

Before Philadelphia, the braids, the sleeve and the MVP trophy, Allen Iverson was a six-foot blur at Georgetown — freak athleticism, fearless scoring, pressure defense, Big East wars and a style that was already his own.

Before Philadelphia. Before the crossover became basketball history. Before the braids, the sleeve, the scoring titles and the culture shift, Allen Iverson was already different. Georgetown just got an earlier version of it — the short fade, the Jordans, the swagger and one of the most explosive six-foot athletes college basketball had ever seen.

Young AI was a freak athlete.

That part of Allen Iverson gets lost now. We remember the craft: the crossover, the pull-up, the scoring angles, the ability to get a shot off against anybody. But Georgetown Iverson could overwhelm a game before the skill package was fully polished because his speed and bounce were ridiculous.

He was not merely quick. He was dumb quick. The first step compressed a defense instantly. In the open floor, one bad angle was enough to turn a normal possession into a layup or a dunk. At roughly six feet and 165 pounds, Iverson caught lobs, attacked shot blockers and played above the rim in a way later generations sometimes forget because years of NBA punishment eventually pushed more of his game below it.

The NCAA Tournament opener in 1996 gave people a perfect snapshot: 31 points on 13-of-18 shooting with highlight-reel dunks mixed in. A week later, against Marcus Camby and UMass, the same mentality was still there. Camby was a 6-foot-11 All-American shot blocker. Iverson still went straight at the rim and finished an early drive with a dunk.

KCDATRUTH TAKE

“People forget how athletic young AI was. He was catching bodies and lobs at six feet. We knew the talent and scoring already — the athleticism is the part that gets forgotten.”

John Thompson gave him more than basketball.

To understand Georgetown Iverson, the story has to begin before the first box score. Following the 1993 bowling-alley case in Hampton, Iverson served nearly four months after a conviction that was later overturned by the Virginia Court of Appeals for insufficient evidence that he acted as part of a mob. By the time he needed a college opportunity, much of the recruiting world had backed away.

John Thompson did not.

That matters because Big John had already made Georgetown. He had a national championship, a national reputation and a program identified with elite Black basketball excellence. He had options. Iverson did not. Thompson still put his credibility behind the young man.

Iverson later said, “Coach Thompson saved my life.” Thompson also understood that giving Iverson a second chance did not mean sanding away everything that made him special. When Georgetown’s pace changed around its electric freshman guard, Thompson explained it in a way that still defines the relationship: “I didn't want him to be incarcerated on the basketball court.”

That is the John Thompson piece that can get overlooked. He was not simply a coach who happened to recruit a superstar. He believed in creating an environment where young Black men could succeed, and he was willing to absorb criticism to do it. Who knows what Allen Iverson’s path looks like if Thompson never opens that door?

KCDATRUTH TAKE

“Coach Thompson was already accomplished. He took a chance on Iverson when Iverson didn’t really have the chance to choose Thompson. He was for the culture and wanted to put Black men in an environment where they could succeed.”

1994–95: the freshman was already a problem.

Iverson arrived and immediately became Georgetown’s leading scorer. As a freshman he averaged 20.4 points, 4.5 assists, 3.3 rebounds and 3.0 steals. He won Big East Rookie of the Year and, even more telling for a six-foot freshman guard, Big East Defensive Player of the Year.

The defense is another part of the Iverson story that often gets flattened by the scoring legacy. College AI hunted the ball. He pressured guards, anticipated passing lanes and turned steals into instant offense. Georgetown had always been built on physical defense under Thompson, but Iverson gave that identity speed.

He was still raw offensively — 39 percent from the floor, 23.2 percent from three and more than four turnovers per game — but that almost made the ceiling more obvious. The polish was not there yet and he was still giving you 20 a night.

The Hoyas finished 21–10 and reached the Sweet 16. Iverson scored 24 with eight rebounds against North Carolina in the loss. By then, it was easy to look at the freshman and see NBA lottery talent.

And Georgetown was not a one-man team.

Iverson became the headline, but Thompson had real players around him. The 1994–95 frontcourt was strong and experienced. Othella Harrington averaged 12.2 points and 6.0 rebounds while shooting 55.9 percent. Jerome “Junkyard Dog” Williams averaged 10.9 points and 10.0 rebounds. Don Reid brought interior defense and experience, and Jahidi White was already developing into the huge body Georgetown could throw into the paint.

That mattered because the Hoyas could still play the bruising Georgetown basketball everyone expected while Iverson pushed the game into another gear.

1995–96: from star to one of the best guards in America.

The sophomore leap is where the story changes. Iverson’s scoring rose to 25.0 per game, but the efficiency jump was just as important. He went from 39.0 percent shooting to 48.0, and from 23.2 percent from three to 36.6. The speed was still the weapon. Now the decisions and shot-making were catching up.

He repeated as Big East Defensive Player of the Year and earned First-Team All-America recognition. Georgetown finished 29–8, went 13–5 in the Big East, ended the year No. 4 in the AP poll and reached the Elite Eight.

And the conference around him was loaded: Ray Allen at UConn, Kerry Kittles at Villanova, John Wallace at Syracuse, Danya Abrams at Boston College. Iverson still led the Big East in scoring.

Victor Page gave the Hoyas another attacking guard.

Freshman Victor Page was not just standing in the corner waiting for Iverson to finish possessions. Page started 32 games and averaged 12.5 points, giving Georgetown a second aggressive perimeter scorer who could create pressure.

That changed the shape of the team. Harrington and Williams gave Thompson two proven veterans up front. Page added another downhill guard. Jahidi White and Boubacar Aw supplied size and depth. Iverson remained the center of gravity, but the 1995–96 Hoyas were a legitimately deep team built to make a run.

The best reminder came in the 1996 Big East championship game. Iverson had a quieter 13-point night. Page led Georgetown with 20 while Williams grabbed 12 rebounds. The Hoyas were good enough to be in control of UConn late without needing AI to score 30.

AI vs. Ray Allen was appointment television.

THE BIG EAST RIVALRY

ALLEN vs. ALLEN

Ray Allen was smooth, surgical and one of the best shooters in the country. Iverson was speed, pressure and controlled chaos. Their UConn–Georgetown battles gave the era a perfect contrast in superstar guards.

The rivalry had chapters. In the 1995 Big East semifinals, UConn beat Georgetown 88–81; Iverson scored 27 and Ray Allen had 24. One year later they met again at Madison Square Garden for the conference championship.

Georgetown led 74–63 with under five minutes left. UConn scored the final 12 points. Ray Allen, after struggling most of the night, hit the off-balance game-winner with roughly 14 seconds left. UConn 75, Georgetown 74.

That was Big East basketball: stars seeing each other again, conference grudges carrying into March, Madison Square Garden feeling like the center of the sport.

KCDATRUTH TAKE

“The UConn battles with Ray stand out. The Big East Tournament battles stand out. College Iverson already had style too — the short fade, the Jordan 11s, the whole presence.”

The style was already there.

Before the cornrows became one of the defining looks in NBA history, there was the short fade. Before the Reebok Question became inseparable from his name, the visual memory of college AI included the Georgetown uniform and Jordans on his feet.

The culture piece did not suddenly switch on when he reached Philadelphia. The presentation evolved, but the individuality was already visible. Iverson already carried himself like himself. He did not look manufactured. He did not play manufactured. That authenticity would become one of the biggest reasons an entire generation connected with him.

March 1996: one win from the Final Four.

Texas Tech showed what Georgetown needed from its star in the biggest moments. Iverson took 29 shots and only made 10, but he kept attacking, got to the line, scored 32 and created five steals. The game was not always aesthetically perfect. His pressure on the entire possession was the point.

Then came UMass and Marcus Camby. The Minutemen were elite, Camby was one of the best players in the country and Georgetown got handled 86–62. Iverson scored 23 in what became his final college game.

No Final Four. No national title. But the résumé did not need either to make the NBA conclusion obvious.

Two seasons were enough.

Iverson finished his Georgetown career with 67 games, 1,539 points and averages of 23.0 points, 4.6 assists, 3.6 rebounds and 3.2 steals. He won Big East Defensive Player of the Year twice and Big East Rookie of the Year once.

After the freshman year, a top-10 draft projection would not have sounded crazy. After the sophomore year, the conversation was different. The size questions were still there. His past was going to be discussed. Teams could debate whether he was a pure point guard or a small scoring guard.

But the talent had become impossible to hide.

And the context makes it even crazier: the class coming with him included Kobe Bryant, Ray Allen, Steve Nash, Stephon Marbury, Marcus Camby, Shareef Abdur-Rahim, Antoine Walker, Peja Stojaković and Jermaine O’Neal.

One of the most loaded drafts basketball had ever seen.

And Philadelphia owned No. 1.

4DK VERDICT

Georgetown did not make Iverson fearless. It gave his fearlessness a stage.

John Thompson gave him opportunity, protection and a place to grow. Harrington, Williams, Victor Page and a real Georgetown roster gave him a team good enough to chase March. The Big East gave him Ray Allen, UConn, Villanova, Syracuse and nightly competition. Iverson supplied the rest: the speed, bounce, scoring, defense, style and refusal to fear anybody bigger than him. After one year, he looked like a future NBA star. After two, even in the class of 1996, No. 1 felt like where he belonged.
