Minnesota's Championship Blueprint: The Wolves Are More Than Anthony Edwards

Chris Finch has built a team capable of surviving without its superstar. But the real championship question is what happens when Anthony Edwards returns to a group that has learned how to win, create and defend without him.

November 19, 2026 · Pregame edition
Portland filmPower 30Knicks preview4DK verdict

Winning without Ant is no accident

There is a difference between surviving an injury and developing a sustainable identity in spite of it. Minnesota's 11–4 start, including an 8–3 stretch without Anthony Edwards, makes the latter argument worth examining. The Wolves have not needed a single replacement to imitate Edwards. Instead, Chris Finch has distributed offensive responsibility among LaMelo Ball, Ayo Dosunmu and Jaden McDaniels while maintaining the defensive structure that gives the roster its floor.

That is why Finch belongs in the early Coach of the Year conversation. It is not merely the wins. It is the way the team has absorbed a change in its hierarchy without reducing everyone else to emergency placeholders.

The Edwards question has changed: availability before reintegration

At Minnesota's November 19 shootaround, Anthony Edwards said he did not know when his surgically repaired right knee would allow him to return. That is an important correction to any analysis that treats a late-December return as guaranteed. The organization may have a recovery expectation, but the player's own comments do not establish a fixed date. Until he is medically cleared, Minnesota's current structure is not a temporary gimmick to discard at the first opportunity; it is the team's actual competitive identity.

Edwards also described remaining involved by sharing scouting information with teammates while sidelined. That does not prove he will immediately embrace a smaller on-ball role, but it gives 4DK a useful distinction: leadership from the sideline and on-court chemistry are related, not identical. When he returns, the first meaningful test will be whether Finch can retain Dosunmu's decisiveness, McDaniels' confidence and Ball's organizational responsibility while restoring Edwards' elite scoring.

Our evaluation must remain conditional: a healthy Edwards raises the team's ceiling, but only if the surrounding roles stay connected and the team does not trade its current ball movement for predictable late-clock isolation. The right question is not when Minnesota gets its star back; it is what version of the offense welcomes him back.

LaMelo's real value is freeing Edwards

The most important question about the Edwards–LaMelo partnership is not whether both can post impressive individual averages. It is whether Ball can initiate advantages that allow Edwards to catch the ball against an already-compromised defense. Minnesota has needed another reliable organizer who can handle pressure, create off the dribble and make defenders react before Ant attacks.

In a healthy lineup, the Wolves should test Ball-led actions with Edwards moving into space, inverted pick-and-roll combinations, and staggered creator minutes. The danger is stagnation: one guard dribbling while the other watches. Finch must keep both involved and retain opportunities for the supporting cast.

Ayo Dosunmu has changed the depth equation

Dosunmu's rise is more than a volume-scoring story. In the 11 games without Edwards entering November 19, he averaged 21 points, 4.8 rebounds and 3.3 assists. His ability to attack downhill, convert perimeter chances and contribute as a secondary playmaker means the Wolves have another guard who can punish a defense that loads up on its stars.

His 30-point performance against Portland on November 17, including seven made threes, was a reminder that Minnesota can generate offense from several places. The tougher question comes later: when Ant returns, can Ayo keep his confidence and efficiency on fewer touches? That is a coaching and rotation problem worth solving, not a reason to shrink his role to nothing.

McDaniels is becoming more than a defensive assignment

Jaden McDaniels already has a compelling case as one of Minnesota's most valuable perimeter defenders. His offensive growth adds another dimension. If he can attack a rotating defense, finish possessions efficiently and make the next pass without sacrificing his defensive work, he becomes an essential playoff piece without needing a superstar usage rate.

His game-winner against Portland on November 17 was the perfect illustration of a broader point: he did not need to dominate the ball to decide the game. He needed to be available, decisive and ready when the opportunity arrived. His value in a playoff series will depend on making those winning contributions consistently, particularly when opponents try to ignore Minnesota's secondary options.

Gobert, Kuminga and the complementary pieces

Rudy Gobert remains the foundation of Minnesota's interior defense. His screening, rebounding and paint deterrence create value even when his scoring is modest. But the Portland rematch also showed why the Wolves need contingency plans: foul trouble restricted Gobert to four first-half minutes, and Minnesota still found a way to win. Another dependable big would help preserve physicality and defensive flexibility across difficult postseason matchups.

Jonathan Kuminga brings downhill athleticism and another capable forward defender, provided he remains disciplined away from the ball. Terrence Shannon Jr. offers scoring aggression, while Bones Hyland can inject pace and perimeter shot creation. The challenge for each is postseason reliability: can he contribute within a tighter rotation without weakening the team's defensive discipline? Depth is only a championship advantage if coaches can trust it under pressure.

4DK roster reality check: the numbers behind the identity

Statistical cutoff: before the November 19 Knicks game, through 15 Minnesota games. The Wolves are 11–4 and average 116.7 points, 48.6 rebounds and 25.4 assists while committing 16.1 turnovers per game. They shoot 47.0 percent overall and 37.0 percent from three, but only 67.8 percent at the foul line. That last number matters: a team trying to win close playoff games cannot treat free throws as an afterthought. The statistical picture is not simply “great defense, enough offense”; it is a high-level team whose shot-making and rebounding help cover genuine possession-management weaknesses.

Ayo Dosunmu: 18.6 points on 52.2 percent shooting and 46.4 percent from three. The efficiency suggests his expanded role has not required indiscriminate shot volume. His 3.5 assists also matter: he can finish a play or keep the advantage moving. LaMelo Ball: 17.7 points and 5.5 assists, but 3.7 turnovers in 27.7 minutes. The creative upside is obvious; the risk is that a difficult pass can undo an entire defensive stop. Jaden McDaniels: 17.1 points, 6.1 rebounds and 3.7 assists on 53.0 percent shooting. This is more than a low-usage stopper's profile. Minnesota should test how often it can make him a second-side decision-maker without asking him to become a primary isolation scorer.

Jonathan Kuminga: 12.4 points, 5.7 rebounds and 2.8 assists; 39.3 percent from three, but 58.7 percent at the line. His shooting has helped spacing, though his foul shooting complicates late-game deployment. Terrence Shannon Jr.: 12.9 points and 2.9 assists across 12 appearances, a meaningful scoring alternative rather than a token bench piece. Rudy Gobert: 11.1 rebounds and 7.6 points; the box score understates his screening and deterrence but his 44.4-percent free-throw mark is a real tactical target. Joan Beringer: 6.6 rebounds and 1.13 blocks in roughly 17 minutes per game. His activity offers a development pathway, not proof that the Wolves can replace Gobert's positioning and communication immediately.

Anthony Edwards: 33.3 points in his four appearances, on 48.1 percent shooting and 40.0 percent from three. The roster's unexpected success without him should sharpen, not diminish, the central question: how can Finch preserve this shared responsibility once the offense regains an elite first option? Minnesota's best championship version is not an Edwards one-man show plus spectators. It is Edwards bending the defense while LaMelo organizes, Ayo attacks tilted coverages, McDaniels punishes help, and Gobert or the young bigs finish possessions on the glass.

Game study: November 17, Minnesota 106, Portland 105

Portland led 105–101 late before Dosunmu's three-pointer cut the deficit to one. In the final seconds, LaMelo disrupted the inbound, Dosunmu recovered the ball and assisted McDaniels' go-ahead layup with 2.6 seconds left. Trey Lyles blocked Damian Lillard's subsequent attempt. The verified sequence matters because it connects several player roles: scoring, defensive anticipation, quick decision-making, finishing and emergency rim protection.

It was not a flawless performance. Minnesota missed 12 free throws, and Gobert was hampered by fouls. The Wolves won anyway. That combination—multiple ways to win, but identifiable weaknesses—is more instructive than pretending the comeback proves every championship question is settled.

What the Portland game asks of the rotation

Portland's pressure exposed an offensive contradiction

The November 17 box score reveals a team that shot extremely well from the perimeter yet struggled to protect possessions. Minnesota hit 18 of 40 threes (45 percent) but committed 20 turnovers. Those numbers cannot establish exactly which coverages caused each mistake without reviewing the film; they do identify the first sequence to study. Did Portland disrupt Minnesota's initial entry? Were the Wolves late recognizing help? Did the second-side action arrive too slowly? A championship-caliber offense needs counters when the first read disappears.

LaMelo's individual line illustrates that tension: 18 points, nine rebounds, two assists and five turnovers. His playmaking talent is not in question. The issue is whether Minnesota can convert his creativity into controlled, repeatable late-game offense. In a postseason series, opponents will deliberately challenge his passing windows and force him to finish possessions without a clean first option.

Ayo's scoring is efficient, but the burden must be sustainable

Dosunmu's 30 came on 11-of-19 shooting and seven makes in 11 three-point attempts. He also played more than 39 minutes and recorded five assists against four turnovers. That is both an impressive shot-making night and a reminder that an emergency offensive hierarchy can ask a lot of a player. The Wolves need his scoring aggression without forcing him into Edwards-level creation volume every night. With Ant healthy, Ayo's most valuable role may be attacking a bent defense, running the second unit for stretches and defending the opponent's dangerous guards.

McDaniels' progression must be judged on both ends

McDaniels scored 22 on 10-of-16 shooting, with five rebounds, four assists, three steals and a block against Portland. The four assists matter as much as the points: his next stage is recognizing when to finish an advantage and when to move it to the next teammate. A true two-way difference-maker does not merely guard stars and hit open jumpers; he keeps the offense connected while handling demanding assignments. We should study his decisions against closeouts, his ability to finish through contact and whether opponents can force him into uncomfortable self-created shots.

The Gobert problem is really a frontcourt contingency question

Gobert played 22:22, scored zero points, grabbed four rebounds and committed four fouls in the same game. That is not proof he has lost defensive value; it is evidence that Minnesota needs a functional alternative when its defensive anchor is limited. Joan Beringer supplied 13 rebounds and four blocks in 25:30, including six offensive rebounds, but shot just one of six from the line. His activity and rim protection merit real evaluation, while his offensive limitations show why the coaching staff must be selective about playoff combinations. Before recommending a trade, 4DK should evaluate whether Beringer's development, Trey Lyles' spacing and Kuminga's versatility can cover the relevant matchups.

Bench production and playoff translation

Bones Hyland scored 13 on four-of-nine shooting and three-of-six from deep in 16:43; Terrence Shannon Jr. added 12 points and four assists in 24:26. Both can help Minnesota survive non-star minutes. The playoff question is different: can they make the correct defensive rotation, avoid costly turnovers and keep the offense organized when the opponent hunts weaker links? Hyland had three turnovers in limited minutes, while Shannon committed one. Those are not definitive evaluations of either player, but they identify the possession-level questions a serious film review must answer.

What we can conclude — and what remains unverified

The box score confirms production, efficiency, foul trouble and turnover patterns. It does not by itself establish specific defensive coverages, communication errors, screen angles or off-ball routes. Our clip review covers the four sequences above. It cannot explain all 20 turnovers. The editorial conclusion is narrower and stronger: Minnesota has enough shot-making, defense and roster depth to win imperfect games, but the same mistakes can be punished in a seven-game series against an opponent with time to prepare.

Where Minnesota can still be attacked

The first vulnerability is availability. Edwards' right-knee recovery and Ball's injury history make the dream version of this roster a projection until the two share sustained court time. The second is half-court execution against switching, physical playoff defenses. The third is the frontcourt's flexibility if Gobert is drawn away from the rim or forced into foul trouble. And the fourth is free-throw shooting: Minnesota entered the Portland rematch at 69 percent from the line, a serious late-game concern.

A targeted frontcourt addition or dependable bench upgrade could make sense, but the Wolves should not sacrifice the chemistry and depth that have kept them competitive just to make a splash. The smartest move may be the one that protects the system rather than replacing it.

4DK Power 30: The confirmed climb from No. 9 to No. 6

We can now connect the film-study argument to the actual approved 4DK editorial ranking. Minnesota was ninth on the November 10 Power 30 and sixth on the November 17 board—a rise of three places. The November 10 working dataset had Minnesota at 8–3 and described a team whose defensive structure, LaMelo Ball's improvisational playmaking and Rudy Gobert's interior influence were helping it survive Anthony Edwards' absence. The November 17 display board retained the approved No. 6 placement and a 10–4 snapshot. Minnesota subsequently beat Portland 106–105 on November 17 to reach 11–4. These are distinct editorial cutoffs, not conflicting rankings to be silently reconciled.

What did the Wolves do to earn that climb? The Dallas blowout showed they can overwhelm an opponent; the Chicago defeat showed the limitations of assuming the defense will always control a game; and the back-to-back Portland victories revealed two distinct offensive answers. Shannon and Gobert led the November 16 formula. Dosunmu, McDaniels and the supporting cast delivered the November 17 escape. A top-six ranking is justified by the breadth of the response, not merely the win-loss column.

4DK's preseason board had Minnesota fifth and asked whether the new Edwards–LaMelo partnership, Kuminga's role and McDaniels' scoring growth could make the roster championship-caliber. The mid-November rise is encouraging but does not resolve the main unknown: the full version of this team has barely played together. Our current No. 6 ranking recognizes what the Wolves have demonstrated without declaring their championship ceiling settled.

Minnesota's 11–4 start is impressive, but lineup availability and matchup-specific compromises determine how much of that identity survives a playoff-style game.

Two more tests on the calendar

Memphis visits November 21 after its eighth consecutive road win. Against a team that just closed out Cleveland away from home, the Wolves should watch how often a stop actually becomes a clean offensive possession. On November 25 at Oklahoma City, the first outlet and Ball’s decisions against ball pressure will matter. Those are two chances to measure progress on the mistakes Portland nearly punished.

4DK's defining question: Can Finch preserve the habits when the star returns?

Our argument is not that Minnesota has become better without Anthony Edwards. It is that the injury has forced a more democratic division of labor, and that experiment has revealed players the Wolves can trust when opponents load up on their superstar. Dosunmu's scoring and McDaniels' expanded offensive role cannot simply disappear when Edwards is cleared. Ball's responsibility must become purposeful rather than a second high-usage isolation track. Finch's job is to preserve the movement, communication and defensive commitment that powered the 8–3 stretch without Edwards while restoring a player who averaged 33.3 points in his first four appearances.

That is a championship-level coaching problem, but not yet evidence of a championship solution. The November 17 Portland win illustrated both sides: Minnesota had enough individual shot-making and defensive activity to survive, but 20 turnovers and 50-percent free-throw shooting are margins that can erase talent in a playoff series. The team's second-ranked defense during Edwards' 11-game absence is a legitimate foundation; a reliable half-court attack against playoff adjustments remains the unanswered question.

The 4DK verdict: Contenders, if healthy

Minnesota does not need Anthony Edwards to prove it can win basketball games. It needs Edwards and LaMelo healthy together to discover whether it can win the four playoff rounds that matter most.

Finch has established the foundation. Ball can relieve Edwards of some creation burden. Dosunmu has shown he can handle more responsibility. McDaniels is developing into a more complete two-way weapon. Gobert still gives the Wolves a defensive backbone, and the supporting cast has gained meaningful experience. Those are real reasons to believe.

The Wolves are not yet a proven championship favorite. But their path to contention is credible: health, continuity, stronger half-court offense and enough frontcourt depth to survive the West's most difficult matchups. This roster has earned the right to be taken seriously.
